# The devil's best tool is good things

*Stewardship · The Sovereign Atlas, Issue 01 (October 2026) · The Sovereign Atlas, from the teaching of Benjamin Riggins*

> Bad things are easy to refuse. The real discipline is turning down good things that point nowhere, and a North Star is how you tell the difference.

Canonical page: https://sovereignheadquarters.com/magazine/issue-01/the-devils-best-tool

## The two-minute version

- Benjamin Riggins teaches that the devil's best tool is not obvious temptation but good things, because good things can be accepted with a clear conscience.
- A good thing is worthwhile in itself; the right thing is the one that points to your North Star.
- Good things rarely look like wrong turns; they are roads a few degrees off, and the gap only shows over years.
- A North Star works as a filter for opportunities, and a short set of discernment questions keeps the Level 2 goals honest before God.
- Stewardship includes attention: every yes spends hours that the right thing will not get back.

In the middle of teaching the North Star, Benjamin Riggins stops to name what works against it. The first answer is the obvious one. "We have to kill distractions," he says, and he lists the familiar ones: a party, drug problems, lust problems.

Then he adds what he calls "a little side note," and it turns out to be the sharpest line in the video.

> What is the devil's best tool to use on you? ... The devil's best tool to use on you is this: good things.

## Good things versus the right thing

Bad things announce themselves. Most people who are serious about their faith and their work already know to refuse them. The refusal can be hard, but it is never unclear.

Good things announce nothing. They look like service, opportunity, generosity, learning, a favor for a friend, a profitable side project. You can say yes with a clean conscience, and that is exactly what makes them dangerous. A week can be full of good things and still go nowhere.

Benjamin's correction is precise:

> Because the only things that you should ever be doing and setting out for yourself to do before you day by day is the right thing. And what is the right thing? It is the right thing is the one thing that points to the North Star.

The difference between a good thing and the right thing is not moral quality. Both can be good. The difference is direction.

## The road a few degrees off

Plate II draws two roads leaving the same point. At the start they are almost indistinguishable. A mile on, you can barely see the angle. Ten years on, they arrive in different countries.

That is how good things work on a life. No single yes looks like a wrong turn. A committee seat, a second offer, a project that would be fun to build. Each one bends the road a degree or two. The drift only shows at a distance, and by then it has a name. Benjamin's name for it is going through the motions:

> If your North Star does not come to pass, you're going to realize something in your day-to-day work. It's going to feel like you're going through the motions.

The motions can be full of good things. That is the trap. And it has a cousin on the achievement side: a calendar of finished projects that never added up to anything. Goals compound only when they build on one another. Completing unrelated tasks produces *busyness without lasting value*: real effort, real output, no accumulated capacity, no direction.

## How a North Star filters

A North Star is not mainly a motivational poster. It is a filter. When it is clear, it does five jobs at once.

| What the North Star gives | What it filters out |
| --- | --- |
| **Direction** | Attractive opportunities that lead somewhere else |
| **Consistency** | Disconnected weeks, projects and seasons that never add up |
| **Meaning** | Hard work that feels like an isolated burden |
| **Patience** | The panic that a slow month means a failed mission |
| **Identity** | Commitments that train you into someone you don't want to become |

There is research underneath the first row. In Edwin Locke and Gary Latham's [35-year summary of goal-setting research](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.57.9.705), one of the core mechanisms is directive: goals pull attention and effort toward goal-relevant activity and away from the irrelevant. A clear goal doesn't just energize you. It narrows what you notice.

Benjamin's test is practical. Look around your inner circle. Look at what you have to do every day, your priorities and responsibilities, your to-do list, your checklist. Then ask whether it points to "the number one desire of your heart being fulfilled before the day you die." If it doesn't, his verdict is blunt: "then I don't see your North Star actually coming to pass."

Two cautions keep the filter healthy. First, it has to include the whole of life. Benjamin applies it to "a job," "a wife," "kids," everything. A North Star that only describes a business will filter out your family. Second, some duties are not optional. Caring for the people God gave you is not a distraction from the North Star; in a well-written one, it is part of it.

## Level 2 under God

For a Christian, the filter runs deepest at Level 2, the six-month to five-year goals. Benjamin is clear about the order: "Of course, if you're a Christian, then everything goes back to God and the Bible." A 25-year North Star is a direction, not a prophecy, and the milestones are where you find out whether you're still on the road God set.

So before a goal goes on the Level 2 list, put it through six questions:

1. **Does this goal reflect biblical values?**
2. **Does it serve people, or does it mainly raise my status?**
3. **Does it honor my responsibilities to my family?**
4. **Am I acting out of love and faithfulness, or out of fear and pride?**
5. **Have I sought wise counsel, and am I open to correction?**
6. **Does the next step require more trust, wisdom or character from me?**

The last question is the quiet one. Good goals tend to stretch the person who pursues them, not just the bank balance.

Then hold the plan with an open hand. "The heart of man plans his way, but the Lord establishes his steps" ([Proverbs 16:9, ESV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Proverbs+16%3A9&version=ESV)). Plan anyway; Proverbs assumes you will. But don't read every success as divine approval or every obstacle as a command to quit. A reward schedule can't guarantee God's guidance. It is a tool for practicing attentiveness and stewardship while you listen.

## Aim high enough

There is a quieter way good things win: when the North Star is set too low. A small North Star makes almost anything look like it fits.

Benjamin teaches that we downplay what God tells us. We hear something great and shrink it into something manageable. "Very few people have made it to the point where they see their North Star as high as God set it out to be for them," he says, "and therefore, when they reach it, they don't even realize they reached it."

Scripture describes a God "who is able to do far more abundantly than all that we ask or think" ([Ephesians 3:20, ESV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Ephesians+3%3A20&version=ESV)). If what we ask and think is always lower than what he intends, the North Star we write down is probably too modest, and a modest North Star lets too many good things through.

## Stewardship of attention

Stewardship usually means money. It also means hours, and the attention inside them. Every yes spends something the right thing won't get back.

Jesus put the scale of faithfulness at the smallest unit: "One who is faithful in a very little is also faithful in much" ([Luke 16:10, ESV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke+16%3A10&version=ESV)). In this system, the very little is one 90-minute block. Spending it on the right thing, again and again, is how a 25-year purpose gets built.

It is also slow, and good things are fast. The side project pays this month; the North Star pays in a decade. That is why Paul's encouragement belongs on the desk: "let us not grow weary of doing good, for in due season we will reap, if we do not give up" ([Galatians 6:9, ESV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Galatians+6%3A9&version=ESV)).

Benjamin keeps returning to the end of a life, on purpose. "If you want to be truly effective in this life," he says, "you need to outlive your flesh." He points to C.S. Lewis, to David's psalms and Solomon's proverbs. Their work didn't outlast them because they were busy. It outlasted them because it pointed somewhere.

A good thing you decline is not a sin avoided. It is an hour handed back to the right one. Say no kindly, say it early, and let the next micro reward and milestone have something real to serve. For the whole system, start with [the framework](/magazine/issue-01/the-3-level-reward-system).

## Do this

List everything that filled last week, mark each item that pointed toward your North Star, and choose one good thing that didn't to decline, kindly and early, this week.

## Questions people ask

**What does "the devil's best tool is good things" mean?**

It means the most effective distraction is not obvious sin, which committed people already resist, but worthwhile activity that pulls them off course. Benjamin Riggins teaches that you should set out each day to do the right thing, defined as the one that points to your North Star. Good things that point elsewhere quietly consume the time your purpose needs.

**How do you tell a good thing from the right thing?**

Ask where it points. In the 3-Level Reward System, the right thing moves you toward your North Star, your 25-year purpose, directly or through a milestone that feeds it. A good thing can be helpful, generous or profitable and still point somewhere else. If it doesn't connect to the North Star or to a clear duty you already carry, it may deserve a polite no.

**How can a Christian discern which goals God is leading them toward?**

There is no formula that guarantees God's guidance, but you can test a goal honestly. Ask whether it reflects biblical values, serves people rather than status, honors your family, flows from love rather than fear or pride, has been put before wise counsel, and requires growth in character. Proverbs 16:9 frames it: we plan our way, and the Lord establishes our steps.
