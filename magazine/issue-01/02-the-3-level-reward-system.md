# The 3-Level Reward System

*Framework of the Issue · The Sovereign Atlas, Issue 01 (October 2026) · The Sovereign Atlas, from the teaching of Benjamin Riggins*

> Carry a 25-year calling without carrying 25 years of weight: one North Star, a ladder of milestones, a reward inside today, all standing on grace.

Canonical page: https://sovereignheadquarters.com/magazine/issue-01/the-3-level-reward-system

## The two-minute version

- The 3-Level Reward System pays every hour of work three times: a 25-year North Star gives it meaning, milestones six months to five years out make progress visible, and micro rewards make today worth starting.
- Underneath all three sits a foundation the system cannot supply: an identity in God that no result adds to and no missed day takes away.
- Its central move is to separate the size of your purpose from the size of today's action, so the mission can grow while the part of it you carry in your attention shrinks to the next 60 to 90 minutes.
- Benjamin Riggins built it after more rest failed to lift a four-hour daily ceiling; within a couple of months he was working ten hours a day, which is his own result rather than a proven or promised outcome.
- The mechanisms inside it are well studied, from goal-setting and self-concordant goals to proximal subgoals and the progress principle, but the system as a whole is a framework to test, not settled science.

**The 3-Level Reward System** is a way of organizing work so that every hour is paid three times: by a 25-year North Star that gives it meaning (Level 3), by milestones six months to five years out that make progress visible (Level 2), and by micro rewards inside today that make the work worth starting (Level 1), all resting on an identity in God that no result can add to or take away.

Benjamin Riggins built it at a wall. "Just a little over a year ago, I would only be able to work for about four hours before I would leave the working place," he says. "It just seemed like it was a four-hour cap." Everyone told him to rest more. He did, and it backfired: "All it did was give me a cleaner slate to be burnt out." What changed things wasn't more recovery. It was an answer to a different question: what is the work *for*, and what does it pay me along the way?

This is the full framework, built out further than the video had time for.

## Four layers, four questions

The system has three levels you build and one you receive. Each answers a different question, and confusion starts when you ask one layer to do another's job.

| Layer | Horizon | The question it answers | What it gives you |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Foundation: God | Always | Who holds my life? | Identity in Christ, grace, wisdom, guidance, trust |
| Level 3: the North Star | About 25 years | Why? | Direction, purpose, legacy |
| Level 2: milestones | 6 months to 5 years | What next? | Structure and visible momentum |
| Level 1: micro rewards | The next 30 to 90 minutes | What now? | A winnable block and something good after it |

The foundation comes first because it changes the weather of everything above it. Jesus' invitation runs in a particular order: "Come to me, all who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest" (Matthew 11:28, ESV), and only then, "Whoever abides in me and I in him, he it is that bears much fruit" (John 15:5, ESV). A branch doesn't produce fruit to earn its place on the vine. It stays on the vine, and fruit follows.

That is the difference between working *for* acceptance and working *from* it. A North Star pursued for acceptance becomes a verdict you drag behind you. The same North Star pursued from acceptance becomes an assignment you get to carry. Benjamin's closing words in the video are about exactly this: "And we could do it from rest now." The Kingdom Stories essay, [What God did on day eight](/magazine/issue-01/day-eight), takes up that foundation in full.

## Separate the size of your purpose from the size of today's action

Here is the move that makes the whole system work. Usually, the bigger the ambition, the heavier each day feels, because the whole mission rides along every morning. This system reverses that. The mission can grow while the amount of it you hold in your attention shrinks to one focused block.

Your purpose can be immense. Your next step can be small. You don't wake up and negotiate with 25 years of obligations; you ask what single meaningful thing you can advance in the next session, and you advance it. "Therefore do not be anxious about tomorrow, for tomorrow will be anxious for itself. Sufficient for the day is its own trouble" (Matthew 6:34, ESV).

## Build it from the top down

The levels are numbered from the ground up, but you build them from the sky down. "We're going to start with number three," Benjamin says. "We're going to see how it actually all works backwards from three to two to one."

A reward for today's work only means something if today's work is going somewhere, so the first question is where you're headed. Benjamin first met the idea in Stephen Covey's *The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People*: "Stephen Covey calls this... beginning with the end in mind."

## Level 3: the North Star

"I would like to think of it as a 25 year goal," he says. "It's something bigger than yourself that you can't see yourself doing alone." And the test is fulfillment, not size: "If there's one thing that would fulfill your desires more than anything else, it'd be this one thing."

Its job is direction. "Your North Star has to be your compass." Your job, your marriage, your kids, your to-do list: all of it should point back. When nothing does, he names the symptom precisely: "It's going to feel like you're going through the motions."

Two distinctions help you find it. The first is **desire, not want**. "A want is something that has to do with lacking. I want this because I don't have this." A desire is different: "A desire is more of something that will fulfill you." Look for the subject where your fire lights up in a way nobody else's seems to. The second is **purpose, not preference**: "It has to be linked to your desires and it has to be linked to the purpose God gave you." Time alone with God clarifies it. "The more time that you spend there, the more that he unveils your purpose to you."

And aim higher than feels comfortable. Benjamin's warning is that we shrink what God says: "Because we downplay what our Father said." His observation is sobering: "Very few people have made it to the point where they see their North Star as high as God set it out to be for them." Paul's doxology points the other way, to the One "who is able to do far more abundantly than all that we ask or think" (Ephesians 3:20, ESV).

His own North Star is an association. "It's going to be called the Sovereign Association": Christian business owners and entrepreneurs gathering, funding and helping one another, with an aim he states without hedging: "it's going to transform the way that the world sees business forever."

### What makes a good North Star

**It describes a life, not just a business outcome.** A strong North Star answers three questions at once: who am I becoming, whom am I serving, and what am I building? Here is one way a Christian entrepreneur might write it:

> Over the next 25 years, I want to faithfully build businesses that create opportunity for others, become a deeply present father and spouse, steward substantial resources generously, develop other leaders, and grow increasingly Christlike in my character.

That gives business a place inside the whole of life instead of letting it become the whole.

**It is genuinely yours.** Kennon Sheldon and Andrew Elliot's research on self-concordant goals, those consistent with a person's developing interests and core values, found that people put more sustained effort into such goals, and reaped greater well-being benefits when they reached them. A North Star borrowed from someone else's highlight reel tends not to hold through a hard year.

**It can be revised.** A 25-year vision is not a 25-year prediction. "The heart of man plans his way, but the Lord establishes his steps" (Proverbs 16:9, ESV). Hold the direction firmly and the route loosely.

**It is written down.** "Write the vision; make it plain on tablets, so he may run who reads it" (Habakkuk 2:2, ESV). A North Star that lives only in your head can't check tomorrow's calendar.

## Level 2: milestones

"Level 2 has to feed into level 3." That one sentence is the whole rule. Level 2 is the middle distance: "Six months, one year, three years, five years, just little goals, just little milestones." A vacation, a car, a book finished or written. What makes something Level 2 isn't its size. It's its direction.

Benjamin's own include the book he's writing and a monthly roundtable launching in January 2027: "It's going to be called AI for Christians at Work." He pushes for creativity here too: "We are his creation who is creative in the image of the creator." A vacation can be pure rest or a week of journaling about your North Star. Same trip, different level of reward.

This bridge may be the most important part of the design. A North Star 25 years away is too far off to give feedback; daily tasks feel small on their own. Milestones are close enough to pursue and big enough to matter.

| Horizon | What it's for |
| --- | --- |
| 25 years | Direction, purpose, legacy, identity |
| 5 years | Major capacity and positioning |
| 3 years | A sustainable platform |
| 1 year | Measurable results |
| 6 months | A major project or capability completed |
| 90 days | One focused campaign |
| Today | The next controllable action |

The research behind the bridge is strong. Edwin Locke and Gary Latham's review of 35 years of goal-setting studies found that specific, difficult goals raise performance more than vague intentions, especially with feedback and real commitment. And in a classic 1981 experiment, Albert Bandura and Dale Schunk had children who were struggling with arithmetic work toward either near-term subgoals or one distant goal. Under the near-term subgoals, the children progressed rapidly and developed both a sense of efficacy and real interest in the work. The distant goal alone had no demonstrable effect. That is Level 2 in miniature.

The test for every milestone is what happens when you check it off. "It should automatically be getting you closer and closer," Benjamin says. If it doesn't, it may be a good thing rather than the right thing, which is the subject of [The devil's best tool](/magazine/issue-01/the-devils-best-tool). Before a milestone goes on the list, ask: does it serve people, or only my status? Does it honor my family? Am I choosing it from love and faithfulness, or from fear and pride?

## Level 1: micro rewards

"Level one is all about today." The question is a practical one: "What can I give myself today?" The answer sits at the end of a focused block of work, decided in advance.

Benjamin's answers are specific, and one of them he calls "one of my cheat codes to working every day." He's a basketball fanatic and a self-described stat freak. "I hit a 90 minute work block. And I'm like, yo. Give me 10, 15 minutes on this game." Or: "Let me write some of my book. Let me go on a drive. Let me go on a walk. Let me spend time with my wife. Let me spend time with my kids." His rhythm: "30, 60, 90 minutes." Treat that as his protocol, a starting point to test, not a number from a lab.

Why does something this small matter? Teresa Amabile and Steven Kramer studied nearly 12,000 daily diary entries from 238 people in seven companies and found that nothing did more for people's motivation and emotions at work than making progress in meaningful work, even small progress. A defined block with a defined finish turns an endless day into a series of finishes you can feel. "This is how I live my life," Benjamin says. "Every single day."

One guardrail, developed in [Build your reward system](/magazine/issue-01/build-your-own-reward-system): a reward is a celebration, not permission to be happy. Your coffee can follow a block. Your children never have to earn access to you.

## How the levels feed each other

Look closely at his micro rewards and something clicks: several of them *are* Level 2 work. Writing the book is a reward at eleven in the morning and a milestone on the five-year list. The reward for today's block moves the project that moves the North Star. Energy flows up.

Direction flows down. The North Star decides which milestones deserve a place; the milestones decide what today's first block is for. And regular review keeps all three honest. When they're aligned, Benjamin says, "we're starting to view work as something that fulfills a greater purpose, bigger than us. It's not just going through the motions day by day."

Each level fails in a predictable way when it stands alone:

| What you have | What it becomes |
| --- | --- |
| A North Star with no milestones | A daydream |
| Milestones with no North Star | A busy shelf of trophies |
| Micro rewards with neither | Treats on a treadmill |
| All three, without the foundation | A large ambition carried as a verdict on your worth |

## Four hours to ten

"I went from four hours of work to 10 hours of work and just a couple of months and rest was not the solution," Benjamin says, to the point where his wife has to tell him to leave the desk because it's family time. He's careful about what that means: "I'm not encouraging overworking." Neither is this magazine. The jump is his own result: one person's experience, not a controlled study and not a promise. [The Briefing](/magazine/issue-01/the-briefing) sets out what the research can and can't say about it.

## What it is not

**It is not a cure for burnout.** The World Health Organization defines burnout as a syndrome resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed, marked by exhaustion, mental distance or cynicism, and reduced professional efficacy. A purpose-linked reward system can address some of those roots: meaningless effort, invisible progress, a future that presses on every hour. It can't fix an impossible workload, money pressure or poor health. Benjamin says so on camera: "We haven't completely killed burnout."

**It is not a replacement for physical recovery.** Living from rest is a spiritual reality; the body still needs sleep and breaks. Jesus himself told his tired disciples, "Come away by yourselves to a desolate place and rest a while" (Mark 6:31, ESV). Live spiritually from rest, work intentionally from purpose, and recover physically according to your needs.

**It is not proven as a whole.** Self-concordant goals, proximal subgoals, progress feedback: the mechanisms inside it are well studied. The exact three-level architecture is a framework, offered for you to test.

## Questions people ask

**What is the 3-Level Reward System?**

The 3-Level Reward System is a framework by Benjamin Riggins that organizes work around rewards at three distances: a 25-year North Star (Level 3), milestones from six months to five years out that feed it (Level 2), and micro rewards inside today's work (Level 1). All three rest on a foundation of identity in God. It was published in The Sovereign Atlas, Issue 01.

**What is a North Star goal?**

A North Star goal is one long-range purpose, roughly 25 years out, that every smaller goal points back to. In the 3-Level Reward System it should describe who you are becoming, whom you serve and what you build, not just a revenue figure. It works as a compass rather than a forecast: the direction stays steady while the route is revised as life unfolds.

**What are micro rewards?**

Micro rewards are Level 1 of the 3-Level Reward System: small, planned rewards taken after a focused block of work. Benjamin Riggins uses ten or fifteen minutes on a basketball game, a walk, a drive, time with his wife and kids, or writing his book. His rhythm of 30, 60 or 90 minutes is a personal protocol, not a scientifically established interval.

**Why do you need milestones between daily work and a 25-year goal?**

Because a goal 25 years away is too far off to give feedback, and daily tasks feel small without a bigger story. Milestones at six months, one, three and five years bridge the gap. Research supports the bridge: in a 1981 study by Bandura and Schunk, children working toward near-term subgoals gained skill, confidence and interest, while distant goals alone showed no demonstrable effect.

**Can the 3-Level Reward System cure burnout?**

No one should promise that. The World Health Organization describes burnout as chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed. A purpose-linked reward system can address some contributors, such as meaningless effort and invisible progress, but unreasonable workloads, money pressure and poor health need real solutions. Benjamin Riggins says it himself: 'We haven't completely killed burnout.'

**Is the 3-Level Reward System only for Christians?**

Anyone can use the three levels, but the framework was built on a Christian foundation. Benjamin Riggins teaches that your North Star should be linked both to your desires and to the purpose God gave you, and that believers work from rest and acceptance rather than for them. That foundation is what keeps a large ambition from turning into a performance-based identity.
