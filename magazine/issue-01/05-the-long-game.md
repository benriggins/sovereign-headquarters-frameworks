# The long game: how 90-minute sessions compound into a life

*Strategy · The Sovereign Atlas, Issue 01 (October 2026) · The Sovereign Atlas, from the teaching of Benjamin Riggins*

> Time alone compounds nothing. Goals that build on each other compound capability, confidence and character, and a single focused session a day adds up to thousands over 25 years.

Canonical page: https://sovereignheadquarters.com/magazine/issue-01/the-long-game

## The two-minute version

- The 3-Level Reward System is built for capability compounding, not time compounding: each finished goal should leave you more skilled, better connected and better equipped for a larger one.
- The compounding loop runs in six steps: meaningful goal, focused execution, visible achievement, skills and reusable assets, greater capacity, larger achievable goals.
- Goals compound only when they build on each other; a stack of unrelated accomplishments produces busyness, not momentum.
- Confidence compounds too: across 61 studies of academic self-efficacy, past performance accomplishments showed by far the strongest unique link to people's belief in their own ability.
- One focused session a day, five days a week, 48 weeks a year, adds up to about 6,000 sessions in 25 years, an illustration of accumulated opportunity rather than a promise of results.

There are two kinds of compounding, and only one of them is on your side by default.

**Time compounding** is the kind everyone knows. Money left in an account grows whether or not you change. You can be asleep and it still works.

**Capability compounding** is different. Nothing grows while you sleep; *you* grow, and so the next goal costs you less than the last one did. It's the kind the [3-Level Reward System](/magazine/issue-01/the-3-level-reward-system) is built to produce, and it's what Benjamin Riggins meant when he described his own jump from four hours of work a day to ten: "the productivity compounds over that." His point wasn't that he had more hours. It was that each hour was now building on the one before.

## The compounding loop

Every cycle of the system runs the same six steps:

1. **A meaningful goal.** A Level 2 milestone that points at the North Star.
2. **Focused execution.** Level 1 blocks, one deliverable at a time.
3. **Visible achievement.** The milestone is finished and you can see it.
4. **Skills and reusable assets.** What the work leaves behind, beyond the result itself.
5. **Greater capacity.** You now know more, can do more and have more to work with.
6. **Larger achievable goals.** The next milestone can be bigger than the last.

Then the loop starts again, from a higher floor. The next cycle begins with more knowledge and capacity than the previous one, provided the earlier work produced something reusable. That condition is the whole game.

## Six months, then six weeks

Finish a six-month goal and you don't only get its result. You may also walk away with a new skill that makes future work easier, a process you can repeat or hand off, relationships and reputation, information that sharpens your next decision, revenue or assets that fund the next project, and confidence that you can execute.

Carry those forward and something striking becomes possible: what took six months in year one may take six weeks a few years later, because you bring better judgment, relationships, systems and experience to it. Engineers noticed a version of this long ago. In 1936 the aircraft engineer T. P. Wright reported that the average labor needed per airplane fell by a roughly constant proportion each time a factory's cumulative output doubled. People and organizations learn by doing, and the learning stacks.

It isn't automatic. That's the honest part.

## Busyness doesn't compound

Goals compound only when they build on each other. Ten unrelated accomplishments can fill a year and leave you exactly where you started: a full calendar, a tired body, nothing that carries forward. That's the difference between busyness and value.

Benjamin's test for a milestone is the same one that makes compounding possible. Once you check it off, "it should automatically be getting you closer and closer" to the North Star. His own Level 2 list is built that way. He's writing a book, and he's starting "a monthly roundtable at the beginning of January or the middle of January of 2027" called AI for Christians at Work. The book clarifies the ideas the roundtable teaches; the roundtable gathers the people the North Star needs. "Eventually," he says, "years down the line, the North Star will be fulfilled."

Before you start any milestone, ask one question: *what will this leave behind that the next goal can use?* If the honest answer is "nothing," it may be a good thing rather than the right thing. [The devil's best tool](/magazine/issue-01/the-devils-best-tool) explains why that distinction matters so much.

## Confidence compounds too

The most valuable thing a finished goal leaves behind may be invisible. Albert Bandura's work on self-efficacy, the belief that you can carry out the actions a situation requires, identified mastery experiences as one of its sources: actually doing hard things successfully. A later meta-analysis by Angela Byars-Winston and colleagues pooled 61 studies of academic self-efficacy, covering 8,965 people, and found that past performance accomplishments had by far the strongest unique association with self-efficacy, ahead of encouragement, role models and emotional state.

In plain terms: you don't talk yourself into confidence. You finish your way into it. A completed 90-day goal makes the six-month goal feel possible. A completed six-month goal makes the annual one feel reasonable. Over years, people become capable of pursuing things they would once have called unrealistic. The person grows alongside the work.

Self-determination theory names why this feels good rather than merely productive. Edward Deci and Richard Ryan's research holds that people have three basic psychological needs, for competence, autonomy and relatedness, and that environments meeting them support both high-quality performance and wellness. The compounding loop feeds competence directly. A North Star you chose yourself supports autonomy. And work that connects to people you love and serve supplies the third.

## A 25-year plan, worked backwards

Here's how the loop looks for a hypothetical Christian entrepreneur whose North Star is to build an ethical, resilient enterprise that creates lasting value, develops leaders, funds generosity and strengthens rather than consumes family life.

| Horizon | The goal |
| --- | --- |
| 5 years | A profitable business model with repeatable systems and leaders who can run them |
| 3 years | A dependable way of winning customers, a trusted reputation, core operating procedures |
| 1 year | A focused offer validated, a stable customer base, better delivery |
| 6 months | The offer launched and refined with real customer feedback |
| 90 days | Prospective customers interviewed, positioning tested, first customers won |
| Today | One focused 60–90 minute session on the most important customer or product outcome |

Read it bottom to top and you see the loop. Every stage produces information and capability that shapes the next. Nothing on the five-year line could be built in one enormous effort. All of it can be built one session at a time.

## The arithmetic of 6,000 sessions

Here is an illustration, not a promise. One focused session a day, five days a week, for 48 weeks a year:

- 5 × 48 = **240 sessions a year**
- 240 × 25 = **6,000 sessions in 25 years**
- At 60 to 90 minutes each, that's roughly **6,000 to 9,000 hours** of focused work

Six thousand is a big number made of a small one. But count isn't the point; direction is. Six thousand sessions on unrelated tasks build very little. Six thousand sessions that each feed a milestone that feeds a North Star can build something that would have been impossible to construct by force. The quality, relevance and adaptability of the work matter far more than the hours.

"One who is faithful in a very little is also faithful in much" (Luke 16:10, ESV).

## Compounding outside the office

The same law works everywhere you repeat something small for a long time. A ten-minute conversation with your child at bedtime, repeated for years. Consistent attention to your marriage. Prayer and Scripture before the first work block. Generosity practiced in small amounts until it becomes who you are. Benjamin is building his system around the people at home, not in spite of them: "I have a wife and I have a kid and I have a family and I'm trying to provide every day."

A 25-year North Star should never deliver a thriving company at the price of a diminished life. That's why the strongest North Stars name a family and a character alongside a business.

And the deepest compounding isn't ours to engineer. Paul describes believers "being transformed into the same image from one degree of glory to another. For this comes from the Lord who is the Spirit" (2 Corinthians 3:18, ESV). We bring the faithful sessions. God does the transforming. That's also why perseverance makes sense on the slow days: "And let us not grow weary of doing good, for in due season we will reap, if we do not give up" (Galatians 6:9, ESV).

## The long game is played today

The paradox of a 25-year plan is that you can only ever work on it today. When the foundation of the rebuilt temple looked unimpressive, the prophet promised that even those who had "despised the day of small things" would rejoice to see the work finished (Zechariah 4:10, ESV). Don't despise the small session. Do the next block well, take its reward, review the week, and let the years add up.

"So teach us to number our days that we may get a heart of wisdom" (Psalm 90:12, ESV). Six thousand sessions is one way of numbering them. ## Do this

Look at your next 90-day milestone and write down the one reusable thing it will leave behind (a skill, a process, a relationship or an asset). If you can't name one, redesign the milestone before you start it.

## Questions people ask

**What is capability compounding?**

Capability compounding is growth that comes from what each completed goal leaves behind: skills, processes, relationships, reputation, confidence and assets that make the next goal easier. It differs from time compounding, where something grows simply because time passes. In the 3-Level Reward System, milestones are chosen so each one builds the capacity the next one needs, so a project that took six months early on may later take six weeks.

**How do goals compound over time?**

Goals compound when they build on each other. A completed goal produces a result plus something reusable: a skill, a system, a relationship, information or money. That raises your capacity, so your next goal can be larger. Unrelated goals don't compound, because nothing carries forward. The test is simple: before starting a milestone, name the lasting asset it will leave behind.

**How many focused work sessions are in 25 years?**

About 6,000, if you complete one focused session a day, five days a week, for 48 weeks a year: 5 × 48 = 240 sessions a year, and 240 × 25 = 6,000. At 60 to 90 minutes each, that is roughly 6,000 to 9,000 hours. It's an illustration of accumulated opportunity, not a guarantee; the quality and direction of the work matter more than the count.

**Does compounding apply outside of business?**

Yes. The same pattern of small, faithful actions repeated over years applies to marriage, raising children, friendships, generosity and spiritual disciplines. A ten-minute conversation with a child, repeated for years, builds something no single weekend can. The 3-Level Reward System treats these as part of the North Star itself, so a thriving company never comes at the cost of a diminished life.
