# Build your reward system before tomorrow morning

*Build · The Sovereign Atlas, Issue 01 (October 2026) · The Sovereign Atlas, from the teaching of Benjamin Riggins*

> The design manual: each level written tonight, an if–then that starts the day for you, rewards that celebrate without bargaining, and a review rhythm that keeps it all pointed true.

Canonical page: https://sovereignheadquarters.com/magazine/issue-01/build-your-own-reward-system

## The two-minute version

- Build the 3-Level Reward System top down tonight: one honest paragraph for your North Star, four milestones that each create something lasting, and an if–then plan for tomorrow's first block.
- If–then plans are among the best-supported tools in motivation research: across 94 tests, Gollwitzer and Sheeran found a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment.
- Design rewards as celebration, not permission to be happy, and never make time with your family conditional on productivity.
- Measure the actions you control separately from the results you don't, and review on four rhythms: daily, weekly, quarterly and once a year for the North Star.
- A missed day is information, not a verdict; in the best-known habit study, missing a single opportunity did not materially affect habit formation.

A framework you only read is a framework you forget. This is the manual for building the [3-Level Reward System](/magazine/issue-01/the-3-level-reward-system) tonight and running it tomorrow. If you'd rather click than write, [the interactive builder](/magazine/issue-01/build) takes you through the same steps in order. Benjamin Riggins puts the first sitting at "a 20, 30 minute process," and he means it as your first win: "That could be your first level 1."

## Tonight, Level 3: one honest paragraph

Most people stall at the North Star because it feels too big for a text box. Write it anyway, in three parts:

- **Who am I becoming?** The character you want at 60, not just the bank balance.
- **Whom am I serving?** Family, customers, a church, a city, a cause.
- **What am I building?** The company, book, ministry or body of work.

Then check it against Benjamin's two filters. Is it a desire, "something that will fulfill you," rather than a want built on lack? And is it linked "to the purpose God gave you"? Write it as a draft, not a vow. A 25-year vision is not a 25-year prediction, and you'll revisit it once a year.

## Tonight, Level 2: four checkpoints that each leave something behind

Set one milestone each at six months, one year, three years and five years. Benjamin's rule: "Level 2 has to feed into level 3."

Add a second test: what lasting asset will this create? A skill, a repeatable process, a relationship, a body of work, savings. Milestones that leave nothing reusable produce busyness, not progress. Then make the nearest one sharp. Edwin Locke and Gary Latham's review of goal-setting research found that specific, difficult goals outperform "do your best," especially with feedback. "Grow the business" is a wish. "Twenty customer conversations by March 31" is a milestone.

## Tonight, Level 1: write the if–then

This is the most underrated step. An implementation intention is a plan that names the cue, the action and the context in advance: *when X happens, I will do Y.* In a meta-analysis of 94 independent tests, Peter Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran found that forming one had a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment (d = 0.65), helping people start, stay on task and protect their capacity for later.

Use this template:

> When I **[finish breakfast and sit at my desk]**, I will work on **[one deliverable]** for **[60–90] minutes** before I open email. Then I will **[take a walk / play 15 minutes of my game / read]**.

The cue does the deciding so your willpower doesn't have to.

## A sample morning

| Step | What happens | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Before the block | Prayer, Scripture, gratitude; name the day's most meaningful action | Start from rest and acceptance, not from pressure |
| 2. The block (60–90 min) | One important deliverable. Notifications off. A written definition of done | A finish line you can actually reach |
| 3. The micro reward | Coffee, a walk, a short game, an unhurried conversation | Celebration and renewal |
| 4. Reconnect | Family, service, other responsibilities, or a second block as the day requires | The block serves the life, not the other way round |

Benjamin's own rhythm is "30, 60, 90 minutes," with "10, 15 minutes" of his basketball game after a 90-minute block. Sixty to ninety minutes is a design choice, not a scientifically established interval. Some tasks and some people do better with shorter blocks. Finishing one block doesn't clear the day's other duties; it gives you a dependable first victory.

## Write the definition of done

A block without a finish line quietly becomes the whole morning. Before you start, write what "done" means in one line you could check off:

| Vague | Done means |
| --- | --- |
| Work on the book | Draft 800 words of chapter three |
| Do marketing | Send five personal follow-ups to last month's inquiries |
| Fix the website | Rewrite the home-page headline and publish it |

If you hit the line early, stop and take the reward anyway; you can start a second block later. If you're nowhere near it when time runs out, the line was too big. Cut it in half tomorrow. That's the review talking, not failure.

## Five rules for rewards

**1. Celebrate, don't bargain.** In a meta-analysis of 128 experiments, Edward Deci, Richard Koestner and Richard Ryan found that expected, tangible rewards made contingent on doing or finishing a task tended to undermine people's freely chosen interest in it, while positive feedback enhanced it. So don't build a system in which you are only allowed to enjoy coffee once you've earned it. Treat the reward as gratitude for progress, and make noticing the progress itself part of the reward. Rewards are reinforcement and enjoyment, not permission to be happy.

**2. Never make your family conditional on productivity.** Benjamin lists time with his wife and kids among the things he goes to after a block, and that's the right spirit: a joy you walk into, not a prize you must earn. On a day when every block goes wrong, you still go home to them. Your children never have to earn access to you.

**3. Pick rewards you can stop.** Ten minutes of something good is a reward. Three hours of it is a new problem.

**4. Let some rewards restore you.** A 2022 meta-analysis of 22 study samples found that micro-breaks gave small but significant boosts in vigor and reductions in fatigue; the overall effect on performance was not statistically significant. Breaks make the day more livable more reliably than they make it more productive. A walk earns its place for that alone.

**5. Let some rewards move Level 2.** "Let me write some of my book," Benjamin says of one of his favorite rewards. When the treat after a block is also a milestone, the levels start feeding each other.

## Measure actions separately from results

You can control whether you make ten thoughtful sales contacts this week. You can't directly control whether ten customers buy. Keep two columns: the actions you committed to, and the results that came back. Reward the first. Learn from the second. Mixing them punishes faithful work in slow seasons and flatters lucky work in fast ones.

## The review cadence

| Rhythm | Time | The question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Daily | 5 minutes | Did I finish today's first block, and did today move a milestone? |
| Weekly | 30 minutes | What filled my week? What do next week's blocks need to be? |
| Quarterly | An afternoon | Did the last 90 days make the next season more fruitful? Which milestone needs adjusting? |
| Annually | A day away | Is the North Star still true? What has God been showing me? |

Keep the purpose stable where it should be and change the route when reality changes. The quarterly test isn't only whether you completed your tasks. It's whether your efforts made the next season more fruitful and your life more aligned with what matters most.

## When you miss a day

You will. Plan for it. In Phillippa Lally's 2010 study of how habits form, people repeating a new daily behavior in the same context reached near-peak automaticity after a median of about 66 days, with individual estimates ranging from 18 to 254. And missing a single opportunity did not materially affect the process.

So a missed day is information, not a verdict. Write a second if–then for it: *if I miss my morning block, then I will do a 30-minute block before lunch.* Start again with the next block, not next Monday. "The steadfast love of the Lord never ceases; his mercies never come to an end; they are new every morning" (Lamentations 3:22–23, ESV). The system measures progress. It doesn't measure you.

## Questions people ask

**How do I create my own reward system for work?**

Build it from the top down. Write a 25-year North Star describing who you're becoming, whom you serve and what you build. Set milestones at six months, one, three and five years that each create something lasting. Then plan tomorrow's first focused block as an if–then plan with a small reward after it. The free builder in The Sovereign Atlas, Issue 01, walks through all three levels.

**What are good examples of micro rewards?**

Good micro rewards are short, genuinely enjoyable and easy to stop. Benjamin Riggins uses ten or fifteen minutes on a basketball game, a walk, a drive, time with his wife and kids, or writing his book. The best ones restore you, like a walk, or advance a bigger goal, like the book. Avoid anything that takes hours or that you can't put down.

**How long does it take for a work routine to become automatic?**

Longer than 21 days, and it varies widely. In Phillippa Lally's 2010 study of daily habits, the median time to reach near-peak automaticity was about 66 days, with individual estimates ranging from 18 to 254 days. Participants repeated one behavior in a consistent context, such as after breakfast, and those who performed it more consistently showed clearer habit formation.

**What should I do if I miss a day of my routine?**

Start again with the next block rather than the next Monday. In the best-known habit-formation study, missing a single opportunity did not materially affect the process. Your worth isn't on the line either: the system measures progress, not your value. Shrink the next session if you need to, take its reward, and treat the miss as information for your weekly review.
